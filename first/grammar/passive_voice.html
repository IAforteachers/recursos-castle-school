01
Core Mechanics

Structure & Formation: to be + Past Participle

In an active sentence, the grammatical subject performs the action (“The chef baked the cake”). In a passive sentence, the object of the active verb moves into the subject position so we focus on the receiver or result of the action (“The cake was baked by the chef”).

New Subject (Receiver) + be (in the right tense) + Past Participle (V3) + [optional: by + agent]
Two Rules That Never Change

1. Only to be changes tense. The main verb always stays in its past participle form (written, built, repaired, seen).
2. Only transitive verbs (verbs with an object) can be passive. Intransitive verbs such as arrive, happen, appear, sleep, or die never have a passive form.

02
Context & Register

Main Uses: Why Speakers Choose the Passive

Good English speakers do not use the passive at random. Choose the passive voice whenever one of these four communicative goals applies:

Use 1

Emphasizing the Action or Object

What happened or who received the action is the main topic, rather than who performed it.

“A new cancer treatment has been approved after five years of trials.”

Use 2

Unknown Agent

We do not know who did the action, so naming a vague subject like “someone” adds nothing.

“Our office window was smashed overnight.”

Use 3

Contextually Obvious Agent

The person or institution responsible is already obvious from context (police, doctors, workers, teachers).

“The suspect was arrested outside the station.” (obviously by the police)

Use 4

Formal & Bureaucratic Tone

Public notices, official rules, scientific reports, and academic writing prefer an impersonal, objective tone.

“All mobile phones must be switched off inside the examination hall.”

When to Keep by + agent vs. Drop It

Drop by + agent when the doer is unknown, obvious, or vague (by someone, by people, by workers).
Keep by + agent only when naming the doer adds essential or surprising credit: “Guernica was painted by Pablo Picasso in 1937.”

Case A

“The letters are delivered at 8:00 a.m. every day.”

Obvious agent: We obviously know postal workers deliver the mail, so adding “by mail carriers” is unnecessary.
Case B

“Passengers are requested to remain seated until the doors open.”

Formal / bureaucratic tone: Official announcements sound polite, authoritative, and objective without saying “We request passengers…”.
Case C

“Wait—my laptop has been taken from the desk!”

Unknown agent & focus on object: The speaker does not know who took it and cares most about the missing laptop.
03
Everyday Spoken English

Informal Passive with get

In everyday conversation, English speakers often replace be with get (get + past participle) to talk about accidents, mishaps, sudden changes, or things that happen to us unexpectedly.

Natural with GET

Actions, Mishaps & Accidents

Use get for dynamic events (often negative or surprising) in informal speech:

  • My phone got stolen on the bus.
  • Drive carefully or you’ll get fined.
  • Two players got injured in the match.
  • We took a wrong turn and got lost.
Never Use GET

State Verbs & Formal Writing

Exclude state verbs! Verbs describing thoughts, feelings, knowledge, or possession cannot take get:

  • know, believe, understand, love, hate, own, respect, remember
  • Correct: The truth is known by everyone.
  • Wrong: The truth got known by everyone.
Crucial Grammar Difference: Negatives & Questions

Because get behaves like a normal main verb, negatives and questions need do / does / did:
Be-passive: “He wasn’t invited.” / “Were you fined?”
Get-passive: “He didn’t get invited.” / “Did you get fined?”

04
Advanced Structures

Reporting Verbs & Infinitive Patterns

Journalists, academics, and formal speakers use passive reporting verbs (say, think, believe, report, know, expect, consider, claim) when reporting rumors, expert opinions, or general beliefs without naming a specific source.

Pattern When to Use It Formula & Example
1. Impersonal It General reports (works for any tense in the that-clause). It + is/was + V3 + that + clause
“It is thought that the castle is 500 years old.”
2. Simple Infinitive Present state or repeated habit at the same time as the report. Subject + is/are + V3 + to + base verb
“The castle is thought to be 500 years old.”
3. Continuous Infinitive An action currently in progress right now. Subject + is/are + V3 + to be + -ing
“The company is said to be developing a new battery.”
4. Perfect Infinitive Past actions that happened before the reporting verb. Subject + is/are + V3 + to have + V3
“He is said to have left the city yesterday.”
5. Passive Perfect Infinitive A past passive event that happened earlier. Subject + is/are + V3 + to have been + V3
“The painting is believed to have been stolen in 1990.”

Quick Reference

Grammar Checklist

  • 1. Golden Rule
    Always pair a form of to be (matching the subject & tense) with a past participle (V3).
  • 2. Negative Contractions
    Both isn’t / wasn’t / haven’t been and is not / was not / have not been are equally valid.
  • 3. Intransitive Verbs
    Arrive, happen, occur, appear, sleep, and die never take an object, so they have no passive form.
  • 4. State Verbs & get
    Never use get with know, believe, own, love, hate, understand, or respect. Use be instead.
  • 5. Past Reporting Actions
    If the reported event happened earlier than the report, use to have + V3 (active) or to have been + V3 (passive).
